Module 1 — Introduction to Workshop Practice

Workshop meaning, types, manufacturing overview, process classification and product quality

1.1 Meaning and importance of workshop practice

Workshop: A place equipped with tools, machines, materials and safety arrangements where components are made, shaped, joined, repaired or inspected.

Workshop practice: Practical training in the use of engineering tools and processes to convert raw materials into useful components safely and accurately.

Importance

  • Connects classroom theory with real manufacturing.
  • Develops skills in measuring, marking, cutting, shaping, joining and inspection.
  • Teaches material and process selection.
  • Builds safety awareness, teamwork, planning and problem-solving.
  • Improves understanding of tolerances, quality, cost, waste and production time.
Exam answer: Workshop practice is essential because engineers must understand how a designed component can be manufactured safely, economically, accurately and with the required quality.

Safety: Use the correct tool, wear suitable PPE, keep guards in place, tie back long hair, avoid loose clothing near rotating parts and never use unfamiliar machinery without instruction.

1.3 Manufacturing: meaning and sequence

Manufacturing converts raw material into a product of required shape, dimensions, properties and finish using labour, tools, machines, energy and processes.

Product need/specification ↓ Design and material selection ↓ Process planning and tooling ↓ Primary shaping (casting/forming) ↓ Machining / joining / heat treatment ↓ Finishing and surface treatment ↓ Inspection and quality control ↓ Packaging and use

Not every product uses every step. The route depends on shape, material, quantity, tolerances, finish, cost and service requirements.

1.4 Classification of manufacturing processes

ProcessDefinition/examplesMain point
CastingMolten metal fills a mould and solidifies.Complex shapes and internal cavities.
FormingShape changes by plastic deformation: forging, rolling, extrusion, drawing, bending.Usually little material removal.
MachiningMaterial removed as chips: turning, drilling, milling, shaping, grinding.Good accuracy/finish; chips are waste unless recycled.
JoiningWelding, brazing, soldering, riveting and fastening.Can be permanent or detachable.
Additive manufacturingPart is built layer by layer, e.g. 3D printing.Useful for complex shapes and prototypes.
Surface finishingPolishing, lapping, coating, painting, plating.Improves finish and sometimes corrosion/wear resistance.
Powder metallurgyMetal powders are compacted and sintered.Useful for selected shapes and controlled porosity.

Primary shaping creates the initial form (casting). Secondary processing changes dimensions/shape (machining/forming). Finishing improves surface or properties (polishing/coating). Bulk forming deforms solid stock (forging/rolling); sheet forming shapes thin sheets (bending/deep drawing).

1.5 Conventional and modern manufacturing

ConventionalModern/advanced
Manual turning, drilling, milling, casting, forging, welding.CNC machining, additive manufacturing, EDM, laser processes, automated inspection.
Often relies more on direct operator control.May use digital control, automation or specialised energy sources.
Can be economical for simple parts and small setups.Can improve repeatability or produce difficult geometry, but may require high investment and specialist skills.

CNC (Computer Numerical Control): A programmed set of instructions controls machine movement. Advantages: repeatability, accuracy and reduced manual intervention. Limitations: setup/programming cost, maintenance and need for trained operators.

Additive manufacturing: builds a part layer by layer from a digital model. Benefits include complex geometry, less tooling and rapid prototypes. Limitations include build time, material restrictions, finishing needs and cost for some high-volume products.

1.6 Relationship between design, material, process and product quality

DESIGN ⇄ MATERIAL ⇄ PROCESS \ | / \ | / PRODUCT QUALITY (dimensions • properties • finish reliability • cost • safety)
  • Design: geometry, dimensions, tolerances, loads and surface requirements.
  • Material: strength, stiffness, ductility, toughness, corrosion resistance, availability and cost.
  • Process: ability to make the shape, accuracy, finish, production rate and likely defects.
  • Quality: conformance to specification, performance, consistency and reliability.

Example: For a complex metal housing, casting may be chosen. The alloy must flow into the mould and meet strength needs; the gating/feeding design should prevent incomplete filling and shrinkage; inspection verifies dimensions and defects.

Likely long question: Explain the relationship between design, material, manufacturing process and product quality with an example. Use four headings and a simple flow diagram.

Module 2 — Engineering Materials, Mechanical Properties, Measurement & Inspection

Materials, stresses, mechanical properties, measuring instruments and quality control

2.1 Classification of engineering materials

GroupCharacteristicsExamples/uses
Metals and alloysOften strong and conductive; can often be formed/machined.Steel, cast iron, aluminium, copper, brass; structures and machine parts.
PolymersUsually low density; many resist corrosion and insulate electricity.PE, PVC, nylon, epoxy; pipes, insulation, housings.
CeramicsUsually hard, heat-resistant and brittle; good wear/corrosion resistance.Alumina, glass, silicon carbide, bricks; refractories and insulators.
CompositesCombine distinct constituents to get a useful property combination.Fibreglass, carbon-fibre polymer, reinforced concrete.

Ferrous vs non-ferrous

Ferrous: iron is the principal constituent; examples are steel and cast iron. Many grades are strong, but corrosion protection may be needed. Non-ferrous: iron is not the principal constituent; examples include aluminium, copper and zinc alloys. They may offer low density, conductivity or corrosion resistance.

Steel vs cast iron

Steel typically has lower carbon content and is generally more ductile/workable. Cast iron typically has higher carbon content, good castability and useful compressive properties, but many grades are more brittle. Steel is used for shafts and structures; cast iron for machine beds, engine blocks and housings.

2.2 Forces, stresses and strain

Force: a push or pull that may change motion or deform a body. SI unit: newton (N). Stress: internal resisting force per unit area caused by external loading.

Normal stress, σ = P / A

P = axial load in N; A = original cross-sectional area. When P is in N and A in mm², stress is N/mm² = MPa.

TypeMeaningExample
TensilePull tends to elongate.Rod pulled at both ends.
CompressivePush tends to shorten.Loaded column.
ShearParallel forces tend to slide layers.Rivet under transverse load.
BendingTransverse load creates bending; one side tends to tension, other compression.Loaded beam.
TorsionalTwisting moment causes shear stress.Drive shaft.
FatigueDamage due to repeated/fluctuating loading.Rotating shaft under cyclic load.
ImpactSudden load causes dynamic response.Hammer blow.

Strain is change in dimension divided by original dimension; it is dimensionless.

Longitudinal strain, ε = ΔL / LHooke's law (linear elastic range): σ = Eε

E = Young's modulus. Hooke's law is used only within the proportional/linear elastic range.

2.3 Mechanical properties

PropertyDefinitionExample / distinction
StrengthAbility to withstand stress without failure.Load-bearing capacity.
StiffnessResistance to elastic deformation.Higher Young's modulus means less elastic strain at the same stress.
ElasticityReturns to original shape after unloading within elastic range.Spring action.
PlasticityAbility to undergo permanent deformation without cracking.Useful in forming.
DuctilityTensile plastic deformation before fracture.Wire drawing; elongation/reduction in area.
MalleabilityPlastic deformation under compression, e.g. into sheets.Rolling/hammering into sheet.
ResilienceAbsorbs energy elastically and releases it on unloading.Spring material.
ToughnessAbsorbs energy up to fracture; relates to area under full stress-strain curve.Resistance to fracture/impact.
HardnessResistance to indentation, scratching or local plastic deformation.Brinell, Rockwell, Vickers tests.

Wear resistanceResistance to material loss from rubbing/contact.Gears and bearings.
Remember: Strength = withstands stress; stiffness = resists elastic deformation; hardness = resists indentation; toughness = energy to fracture; ductility = tensile plastic deformation; malleability = compressive plastic deformation.

2.4 Stress–strain curve

Stress σ ↑ U = ultimate tensile stress | /\ | / \ F = fracture | Y = yield ______/ \__ | / | / P = proportional limit | / |_/________________________________→ Strain ε O Elastic region: mostly returns on unloading. Plastic region: permanent deformation remains.
  • Proportional limit: stress proportional to strain.
  • Elastic limit: highest stress from which material substantially returns to original shape after unloading.
  • Yield strength: onset or specified measure of plastic deformation.
  • UTS: maximum engineering stress on the tensile-test curve.
  • Fracture: specimen breaks.

Some materials have no sharp yield point; a specified proof stress such as 0.2% offset may be used.

2.5 Measurement, accuracy, precision, least count and errors

Measurement compares a quantity with an accepted standard. Accuracy means closeness to the accepted value; precision means repeated readings are close to each other. Readings can be precise but inaccurate if there is a systematic bias.

TermMeaning
Least countSmallest increment an instrument can read/resolve.
ResolutionSmallest displayed or readable change.
Systematic errorConsistent bias from zero error, calibration, method or environment.
Random errorUnpredictable variation between readings.
Gross errorHuman mistake in reading or recording.
Zero errorInstrument reads non-zero when actual quantity is zero.
Mean, x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) / nAbsolute error = |measured value − accepted value|Percentage error = (absolute error / accepted value) × 100%

Repeat readings to reduce random variation. Calibration and zero correction address systematic errors. Do not report more decimal places than the instrument supports.

2.6 Measuring instruments

Steel rule

Simple graduated tool for linear dimensions. It is quick but limited by graduation, edge condition, alignment and parallax. Align the rule with the dimension and view the scale straight on.

Vernier caliper

Can measure external dimensions, internal dimensions and depth (depending on design). It has a main scale and a sliding vernier scale.

LC = 1 main-scale division − 1 vernier-scale divisionReading = main-scale reading + (coinciding vernier division × LC)

Apply zero correction when required. With the usual convention, correction is the negative of zero error; check the instrument's stated convention.

Micrometer screw gauge

Measures small thickness/diameter using a screw. Parts include frame, anvil, spindle, sleeve/barrel, thimble and ratchet stop.

Pitch = spindle travel in one full rotationLC = pitch / number of thimble divisionsReading = sleeve reading + (thimble division × LC)

Use the ratchet gently to apply consistent measuring force; do not overtighten.

Try-square

Checks whether two surfaces meet at approximately 90°. Mainly a checking/marking tool, not a precision angle-measuring instrument.

Inspection and quality control

Inspection checks dimensions, geometry, surface condition and defects against a drawing/specification. Quality control monitors the process and inspects outputs to reduce non-conforming products. Check calibration, zero, clean surfaces, alignment and repeatability.

Module 3 — Foundry and Casting Practice

Casting principle, patterns, moulding sand, tools, gating, cores, solidification and defects

3.1 Casting: definition, steps, advantages and limitations

Casting is a process in which molten material is poured into a mould cavity, allowed to solidify and removed as a component.

Sand casting steps

  1. Study drawing and select material/process.
  2. Choose or make pattern with necessary allowances and core prints.
  3. Prepare moulding sand and ram around pattern in flask.
  4. Withdraw pattern to form the cavity.
  5. Prepare/place core if internal passages are required.
  6. Form pouring basin, sprue, runner, gates, vents and riser.
  7. Close and clamp mould; check alignment.
  8. Melt metal and pour at a suitable temperature/rate.
  9. Allow solidification/cooling and shake out.
  10. Cut gates/risers, clean, fettle, inspect and finish.

Advantages

  • Produces complex shapes and internal cavities.
  • Suitable for many metals and a wide range of component sizes.
  • May reduce machining and assembly.
  • Sand casting can be economical for prototypes and low/medium quantities.

Limitations

  • Porosity, shrinkage, inclusions, cracks or incomplete filling may occur.
  • Surface finish/accuracy may be lower than precision machining.
  • Molten metal requires strict safety controls.
  • Some parts need substantial finishing and inspection.

Applications: engine blocks, pump housings, machine beds, valve bodies, pipes, impellers and brackets.

3.2 Patterns: purpose, types and materials

Pattern: a replica/model used to create the mould cavity. It is not always exactly the final casting size because allowances and core prints may be included.

Functions

  • Forms the cavity with the desired shape and dimensions.
  • Provides core prints to locate/support cores.
  • Includes allowances for shrinkage, machining, draft and possible distortion.
  • Supports repeatable mould production.
TypeDescription/use
Single-piece/solidOne piece; simple shapes and small production runs.
Split/two-pieceTwo halves meet at a parting line; easier withdrawal for complex shapes.
Loose-pieceDetachable parts help withdraw projections without damaging mould.
Match-plateCope and drag halves mounted on opposite sides of a plate; repeat production.
Cope-and-drag plateHalves mounted on separate plates, often for larger production.
Gated patternMultiple patterns connected to a common gating system.
SweepTemplate rotated around an axis to generate a large axisymmetric cavity.
SkeletonOpen framework guides sand shaping for large/simple castings.

Pattern materials

Wood: light, cheap and easy to shape; may absorb moisture, wear or distort. Metal: durable and stable but heavier/costlier. Plastic/resin: good finish and moisture resistance; cost varies. Plaster: can form intricate low-volume patterns but may be fragile. Choose by quantity, size, accuracy, shape, wear and cost.

3.3 Pattern allowances

AllowanceMeaning and purpose
Shrinkage/contractionExtra pattern size compensates for contraction during cooling/solidification; generally a positive addition.
Machining/finishingExtra metal left on surfaces to be machined after casting.
Draft/taperTaper on vertical faces helps withdraw pattern without damaging mould.
Distortion/camberPattern is shaped opposite to expected warping of the casting.
Rapping/shakeTapping loosens pattern and enlarges cavity slightly; often treated as a negative allowance.
Simplified pattern dimension ≈ casting dimension × (1 + shrinkage fraction) + machining allowance

Be clear whether machining allowance is total for the dimension or per side. If per side, add it to both relevant sides. Draft and distortion are handled separately. Exact shrinkage values depend on alloy and foundry practice.

3.4 Moulding sand: types and properties

Moulding sand forms the cavity around the pattern. It commonly contains sand grains, binder/clay, moisture and optional additives.

TypeUse
Green sandMoist sand/binder mixture used in the moist state.
Dry sandMould is dried/baked before pouring for selected applications.
Facing sandPlaced next to the cavity surface; contacts molten metal.
Backing/floor sandSupports facing sand and fills rest of flask.
Parting sandPrevents sticking between pattern and sand or between mould halves.
Core sandPrepared to make cores for internal cavities/passages.

Properties

  • Refractoriness: withstands high temperature without fusing.
  • Permeability: lets gas/steam escape.
  • Strength: holds shape during handling and pouring.
  • Plasticity/flowability: packs around pattern and reproduces details.
  • Cohesiveness: grains bind together.
  • Adhesiveness: sand adheres to flask walls.
  • Collapsibility: breaks down after solidification to allow contraction and easier shakeout.
  • Fineness: affects finish and permeability; excessively fine sand may impede gas escape.

Balance is important: low permeability can trap gas; low strength can cause mould erosion; poor collapsibility can contribute to hot tearing.

3.6 Pouring and gating system

Gating system is the network of passages that carries molten metal from the pouring point into the mould cavity. Good design aims for smooth filling, less turbulence/erosion, reduced air aspiration and fewer inclusions.

Pouring basin/cup | Sprue | Sprue well └── Runner ── Gate(s) ── Mould cavity | Riser/feeder
PartFunction
Pouring basin/cupReceives metal and directs it into sprue.
SprueVertical passage carrying metal downward.
Sprue wellReduces flow velocity/turbulence at sprue base.
RunnerHorizontal passage distributes metal toward gates.
Gate/ingateOpening from runner into cavity; controls entry.
Riser/feederLiquid-metal reservoir feeding contraction during solidification.
VentAllows gas/steam to escape.

Types of gates

Top gate: enters from above; simple but may cause splashing/turbulence. Bottom gate: cavity fills upward and may reduce free-fall disturbance; filling/feeding must be designed well. Parting-line gate: enters at parting plane and is convenient in many moulds.

Important distinction: gating passages transport metal; a riser feeds liquid metal as casting contracts. A riser should remain liquid longer than the region it feeds.

3.7 Cores, core prints and directional solidification

Core: shaped insert placed inside the mould to create internal holes, passages or cavities that the pattern alone cannot form. Core print: a seat/projection on the pattern that locates and supports the core. Core position, strength and gas venting are important.

Solidification: heat leaves the molten metal through the mould and metal changes from liquid to solid. The solidification pattern influences grain structure, shrinkage and defects.

Directional solidification: encourage solidification to progress from regions farthest from the feeder toward the riser so liquid metal remains available to feed later-solidifying regions. Riser placement, section thickness, chills and mould conditions help control this.

Chills: metal inserts placed at selected locations to extract heat quickly and encourage the desired solidification sequence.

Chvorinov's rule

tₛ = Cₘ (V/A)ⁿ

tₛ = solidification time; Cₘ = mould constant; V = casting volume; A = surface area in contact with mould; n is often approximated as 2 in introductory problems. A larger V/A (modulus) generally means longer solidification time. A riser is commonly designed with a larger modulus than the region it feeds, with a suitable margin.

3.8 Casting yield

Casting yield is the percentage of total poured metal that becomes the useful casting. Metal in sprue, runners, gates and riser is not counted as useful casting mass, though it may be recycled.

Casting yield (%) = (useful casting mass / total mass poured) × 100

A higher yield reduces metal tied up in the gating/feeding system, but adequate feeding and sound casting quality must not be sacrificed.

3.9 Casting defects: causes and remedies

DefectCause/appearancePrevention
Blowholes/gas porosityRounded cavities due to trapped gas, excess moisture or poor venting.Control moisture, improve permeability/venting, dry cores as required and avoid turbulence.
Shrinkage cavity/porosityLiquid metal does not feed contraction during solidification.Correct riser size/location, directional solidification and chills where appropriate.
MisrunCavity not completely filled; metal freezes too early.Suitable pouring temperature/rate, better gating, avoid very thin sections/long flow paths.
Cold shutTwo streams meet but fail to fuse properly.Improve temperature, flow and gate arrangement.
Hot tear/crackCrack during late solidification/contraction when casting is restrained.Improve collapsibility, section uniformity and reduce restraint/stress concentration.
Sand inclusion/scabSand/mould material is embedded in casting.Improve mould strength, ramming and surface; reduce erosion/turbulence.
Metal penetration/burn-onMetal enters sand voids or fuses to mould surface.Suitable sand fineness/refractoriness/coating and compaction.
Mismatch/mould shiftCope and drag are misaligned.Align locating pins; close and clamp correctly.
Flash/finsThin metal projection at joint/parting line.Improve mould joint, clamping and mould condition.
Warpage/distortionShape changes from uneven cooling/residual stress.Uniform section design, suitable cooling/support and pattern compensation.
InclusionsSlag/oxide/non-metallic matter trapped in metal.Clean melt, skim appropriately and reduce turbulent flow.

For a 5-mark answer, define the defect, write two causes and two prevention methods. A small sketch is useful for shrinkage cavity, blowhole, misrun or cold shut.

Solved Numericals

Formula → substitution → calculation → answer

1. Normal stress

Question: A rod of area 200 mm² carries a tensile load of 20 kN. Find stress.

Given P = 20,000 N, A = 200 mm².

σ = P/A = 20,000/200 = 100 N/mm² = 100 MPaAnswer: 100 MPa.

2. Longitudinal strain

Question: A 2 m bar elongates by 1 mm.

L = 2000 mm; ε = ΔL/L = 1/2000 = 0.0005 = 5 × 10⁻⁴Answer: strain = 0.0005 (dimensionless).

3. Young's modulus

Question: Stress = 150 MPa and strain = 0.00075 in the linear elastic range.

E = σ/ε = 150/0.00075 = 200,000 MPa = 200 GPaAnswer: 200 GPa.

4. Percentage measurement error

Question: Accepted length = 50.0 mm; measured = 49.6 mm.

Absolute error = |49.6 − 50.0| = 0.4 mmPercentage error = (0.4/50.0) × 100 = 0.8%Answer: 0.4 mm and 0.8%.

5. Vernier caliper

Question: LC = 0.02 mm, main-scale reading = 12 mm, 7th vernier division coincides; ignore zero error.

Reading = MSR + (division × LC) = 12 + 7(0.02) = 12.14 mmAnswer: 12.14 mm.

6. Micrometer screw gauge

Question: Pitch = 0.5 mm, 50 thimble divisions, sleeve reading = 6.5 mm, thimble reading = 28.

LC = 0.5/50 = 0.01 mmReading = 6.5 + 28(0.01) = 6.78 mmAnswer: 6.78 mm.

7. Shrinkage allowance

Question: Required casting dimension = 100 mm, shrinkage = 1%, no machining allowance.

Pattern size = 100(1 + 0.01) = 101 mmAnswer: 101 mm.

8. Shrinkage plus machining allowance

Question: Finished length = 200 mm; shrinkage = 1%; machining allowance = 2 mm on each end.

First add total machining allowance: 200 + 2 + 2 = 204 mm.

Pattern length ≈ 204 × 1.01 = 206.04 mmAnswer: approximately 206.04 mm. Actual values depend on alloy and allowance convention.

9. Casting yield

Question: Total poured metal = 12 kg; useful casting = 9 kg.

Yield = (9/12) × 100 = 75%Answer: 75%.

10. Chvorinov's rule

Question: Cₘ = 4 min/cm², V/A = 1.5 cm, n = 2.

tₛ = Cₘ(V/A)² = 4(1.5)² = 9 minAnswer: 9 minutes.

11. Compare solidification time

Question: Same mould constant, n = 2; A has V/A = 2 cm, B has V/A = 3 cm.

tB/tA = (3/2)² = 9/4 = 2.25Answer: B takes 2.25 times as long.

12. Cube modulus

Question: Cube side = 4 cm. Find volume, surface area and V/A.

V = a³ = 4³ = 64 cm³A = 6a² = 6(16) = 96 cm²V/A = 64/96 = 0.667 cmAnswer: 64 cm³, 96 cm², modulus ≈ 0.667 cm.

13. Mean measurement

Question: Readings are 10.2, 10.4 and 10.3 mm.

Mean = (10.2 + 10.4 + 10.3)/3 = 10.3 mmAnswer: 10.3 mm.

14. Force from stress

Question: Area = 80 mm²; tensile stress = 125 MPa.

P = σA = 125 N/mm² × 80 mm² = 10,000 N = 10 kNAnswer: 10 kN.
